### Question 11
**4] Widespread resistance of malarial parasites to drugs like chloroquine has prompted attempts to develop a malaria vaccine to combat malaria. Why is it difficult to develope an effective malaria vaccine? [2010]**


a) Malaria is caused by several species of Plasmodium.

b) Man does not develop immunity to malaria during natural infection.

c) Vaccines can be developed only against bacteria.

d) Man is only an intermediate host and not the definitive host.

**Answer:**
Ans: 
a) Malaria is caused by several species of Plasmodium.

**Explanation:**
Developing an effective malaria vaccine is challenging primarily because malaria is caused by several species of the Plasmodium parasite (option 
a). Each species presents its own unique set of challenges for vaccine development. Plasmodium falciparum, the most deadly species, is particularly adept at evading the human immune system with its complex life cycle involving multiple stages in both mosquitoes and humans.

Another hurdle is the lack of complete immunity in individuals naturally infected with malaria (option 
b). While some degree of immunity can develop over time with repeated exposure, it is often not sufficient to provide full protection against the disease. This incomplete immunity complicates vaccine development efforts as the mechanisms of naturally acquired immunity are not fully understood.

Contrary to option c, vaccines are not limited to bacteria; they can be developed against viruses, parasites, and other pathogens.

Lastly, option d is inaccurate; humans are the definitive host for Plasmodium species that infect them. The complexity of the parasite’s life cycle and its ability to adapt to different environments present significant challenges in developing a vaccine that provides durable and broad protection against malaria.
